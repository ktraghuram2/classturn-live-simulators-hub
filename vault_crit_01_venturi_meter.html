01 / Aim

To determine the coefficient of discharge of a venturimeter by measuring theoretical and actual volumetric flow rates across calibrated differential manometers.

02 / Apparatus

  • Venturimeter (Inlet diameter d1 = 25 mm, throat diameter d2 = 12.5 mm, converging angle 21°, diverging angle 7°) — Constriction device creating a pressure drop across converging and throat sections to determine flow rate
  • CCl4 Differential Manometer (U-tube differential manometer with carbon tetrachloride (density 1590 kg/m³), range 0–50 cm) — High-sensitivity differential head measurement for low to moderate flow rates
  • Hg Differential Manometer (U-tube differential manometer with mercury (density 13600 kg/m³), range 0–50 cm) — Differential head measurement for high flow velocities up to maximum discharge
  • Rotameter (Variable-area tapered glass flowmeter, range 0–50 L/min (0–0.000833 m³/s), precision ±1% FS) — Direct visual measurement of actual volumetric flow rate
  • Centrifugal Pump (0.5 HP monobloc pump, 2800 RPM, coupled with stainless steel sump reservoir) — Provides continuous circulation of water through the closed-loop pipeline circuit
  • Bypass Valve (Globe valve, 25 mm nominal bore, recirculation return line to sump (0–100%)) — Modulates line delivery pressure and bypasses excess fluid to protect the pump
  • Main Control Valve (Precision globe valve, 25 mm nominal bore, graduated handwheel control (0–100%)) — Throttles and sets the primary flow rate passing through the venturimeter

03 / Principles

A venturimeter is a differential pressure flowmeter operating on the principle of conservation of energy as expressed by Bernoulli's theorem and the continuity equation. When an incompressible fluid flows through the converging cone into the constricted throat section, its velocity increases in accordance with the continuity relation. By conservation of mechanical energy, this kinetic energy increase causes a simultaneous drop in static pressure at the throat. Measuring this differential pressure head between the inlet and the throat enables the theoretical volumetric flow rate to be calculated. In real fluid flow, viscous boundary layer friction and turbulence cause minor energy dissipation, resulting in an actual discharge slightly less than the theoretical discharge. The ratio of the actual volumetric discharge (indicated directly by the rotameter) to the theoretical volumetric discharge yields the coefficient of discharge (Cd). For a standard streamlined venturimeter operating at high Reynolds numbers, Cd typically ranges from 0.95 to 0.98.

  • Continuity Equation:
    States that the volumetric flow rate remains constant along a steady, incompressible streamline
    Q=A1v1=A2v2
    Where:
    • Q : Volumetric flow rate (m3/s)
    • A1 : Cross-sectional area of inlet pipe (m2)
    • v1 : Mean fluid velocity at inlet section (ms)
    • A2 : Cross-sectional area of throat (m2)
    • v2 : Mean fluid velocity at throat section (ms)

04 / Procedure

The experiment is conducted on a closed-circuit hydraulic test rig. Water from the sump tank is circulated by the centrifugal pump through the pipeline test section housing the venturimeter. Downstream discharge is adjusted by manipulating the main control valve and bypass valve, with the actual flow rate indicated continuously by the rotameter. Differential pressure tappings at the inlet and throat connect to either a CCl4 manometer (for low flow sensitivity) or an Hg manometer (for high flow rates). After purging trapped air bubbles, corresponding readings of discharge and manometer deflection are recorded to evaluate Cd.

  1. Step 1: Check the pipeline for leaks and ensure the sump reservoir is filled with clean water to the operating level.
  2. Step 2: Start the centrifugal pump with the bypass valve fully open and the main control valve completely closed.
  3. Step 3: Select the appropriate manometric fluid (CCl4 for low flow sensitivity or Hg for higher flow capacity) and purge air bubbles from the connecting pressure lines.
  4. Step 4: Gradually open the main control valve while adjusting the bypass valve to establish the desired flow rate indicated on the rotameter.
  5. Step 5: Allow the flow to reach steady state, then record the actual discharge Qact from the rotameter and the left and right meniscus levels of the manometer.
  6. Step 6: Compute the manometer deflection x = |h1 - h2|, evaluate differential head h, and determine theoretical discharge Qth.
  7. Step 7: Calculate the coefficient of discharge Cd = QactQth for the current flow setting.
  8. Step 8: Repeat measurements across a series of increasing flow rates, tabulate all values, and plot actual discharge against theoretical discharge to find the average Cd.

07 / Validation and limits

  • Assumption: The working fluid (water) is incompressible, homogeneous, and isothermal.
  • Assumption: Flow through the venturimeter is steady, continuous, and one-dimensional along the pipe centerline.
  • Assumption: Frictional shear losses across the converging cone are negligible compared to total pressure drop.
  • Assumption: The venturimeter axis is strictly horizontal, ensuring equal elevation datum head (z1 = z2).
  • Assumption: No pump or hydraulic energy source is situated between the upstream inlet and throat measurement stations.
  • Boundary Limit: Applicable for turbulent pipe flow regimes with Reynolds numbers between 104 and 106 where Cd remains constant around 0.96 to 0.98.
  • Boundary Limit: The CCl4 manometer operates up to a maximum head deflection of 45 cm; higher flow rates require switching to the Hg manometer.
  • Boundary Limit: Cavitation risk occurs if throat static pressure drops below fluid vapor pressure under excessive flow velocities.
  • Boundary Limit: A straight unobstructed upstream pipe length of at least 10 pipe diameters is required to maintain a fully developed velocity profile.
