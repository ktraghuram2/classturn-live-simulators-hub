01 / Aim

To study the photoelectric effect, observe the variation of photocurrent with collector plate potential, light intensity, and frequency, and determine Planck's constant and the work function of target metals.

02 / Apparatus

  • Tunable Monochromatic Light Source (Continuously variable wavelength λ=100–750 nm, adjustable intensity 10–100 W/m², quartz collimator) — Projects monochromatic radiation of selected frequency and intensity onto the photo-emissive metal cathode
  • Evacuated Quartz Phototube & Metal Plate (Target) (High-vacuum quartz envelope (< 10−6 Torr) housing interchangeable metal target cathodes (Cs: 2.14 eV, K: 2.30 eV, Zn: 4.31 eV) and facing collector anode) — Houses the photosensitive target metal plate that emits photoelectrons and the anode plate that collects them

03 / Principles

The photoelectric effect is the instantaneous emission of electrons from a clean metal surface when electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency illuminates it. According to Einstein's photoelectric theory, light interacts with matter as discrete energy quanta called photons, each carrying energy E=h·ν, where h is Planck's constant and ν is the radiation frequency. When a photon strikes an electron near the metal surface, it transfers its entire energy in a 1-to-1 interaction. A minimum energy, termed the work function W=h·ν0, is consumed to liberate the electron from the metal lattice; any remaining energy appears as the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectron: Kmax=h·ν−W. In an evacuated phototube, emitted photoelectrons migrate from the emitter cathode to the collector anode, producing a photocurrent I. Applying an opposing (negative) potential retards the electron flow until, at a critical retarding potential known as the stopping potential V0, even the fastest photoelectrons possessing Kmax are turned back, reducing the photocurrent strictly to zero (e·V0=Kmax). By measuring V0 across various frequencies ν, Einstein's equation takes the linear form V0=(he)ν−We. Plotting V0 against ν yields a straight line whose slope provides Planck's constant (h=e·slope) and whose horizontal intercept gives the threshold frequency ν0.

04 / Procedure

The practical determination of Planck's constant involves systematically adjusting the wavelength and intensity of incident radiation falling upon an evacuated phototube, varying the opposing retarding potential between the cathode and anode, and locating the exact stopping potential where the microammeter reading drops to zero.

  1. Step 1: Inspect the optical alignment of the monochromatic light source and verify zero-calibration of the digital microammeter and voltmeter.
  2. Step 2: Select the target cathode material (e.g., Cesium, Potassium, or Zinc) with a defined work function.
  3. Step 3: Set the incident light intensity to a constant baseline value (e.g., 50 W/m²) and adjust the light wavelength to 380 nm.
  4. Step 4: Gradually increase the negative retarding voltage using the fine potential divider until the photocurrent registered on the microammeter drops precisely to 0.00 μA.
  5. Step 5: Record the applied stopping potential V0 displayed on the digital voltmeter.
  6. Step 6: Repeat measurements for multiple wavelengths across the ultraviolet and visible spectrum (100 nm to 550 nm).
  7. Step 7: Plot the stopping potential V0 against incident frequency ν; determine Planck's constant from the slope (h=e · slope) and verify against standard physical literature.

07 / Validation and limits

  • Assumption: Instantaneous emission: Electron emission occurs within 10−9 seconds of photon absorption without thermal delay.
  • Assumption: One-to-one photon interaction: Each absorbed photon interacts with strictly one conduction electron transferring its entire energy.
  • Assumption: High-vacuum condition: Residual gas scattering and collision losses between electrodes are negligible inside the evacuated phototube.
  • Assumption: Uniform planar field: Electrostatic field distribution between cathode and anode plates is approximated as uniform.
  • Boundary Limit: Threshold constraint: Photoelectric emission strictly ceases when incident radiation frequency falls below threshold frequency (ν<ν0).
  • Boundary Limit: Stopping saturation: Photocurrent drops strictly to zero when retarding potential V≤−V0, regardless of light intensity.
  • Boundary Limit: Kinetic energy independence: Maximum kinetic energy depends solely on radiation frequency and cathode work function, remaining invariant with intensity.
  • Boundary Limit: Thermal kinetic dispersion: Practical contact potential differences and slight thermal velocity distributions cause a minor knee near zero current.
