01 / Aim

To record the fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of fluorescein solution using a spectrofluorimeter and determine the optimum excitation and emission wavelength maxima.

02 / Apparatus

  • Spectrofluorimeter (Dual-grating monochromator system with Xenon arc lamp source, light-tight sample chamber, and photomultiplier tube detector) — Generates monochromatic excitation light, isolates sample fluorescence at right angles, and measures emission intensity across 200–900 nm
  • Quartz Cuvette (All-side transparent optical quartz cell, 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm internal optical path length) — Contains the liquid sample allowing 90° orthogonal excitation and emission light collection without ultraviolet absorption
  • Precision Micropipette (Adjustable volume 1.0–5.0 mL capacity with disposable polypropylene tips) — Transfers exactly 3.0 mL of prepared fluorescein solution into the optical quartz cuvette
  • Volumetric Flask (Class A calibrated borosilicate glass volumetric flask, 100 mL capacity) — Holds the prepared stock standard solution of fluorescein in dilute alkaline buffer
  • Computer Monitor & Data Station (High-resolution laboratory computer terminal interfaced to spectrofluorimeter electronics) — Controls automated monochromator wavelength drive, records PMT detector telemetry, and plots spectral curves in real time

03 / Principles

Fluorescence spectroscopy investigates the photoluminescent emission of light from electronically excited singlet states of fluorophores. When a molecule absorbs an incident photon of wavelength λex, an electron transitions from the ground electronic singlet state S0 to an excited vibrational level of S1 or S2. Following ultrafast non-radiative internal conversion and vibrational relaxation to the lowest vibrational level of S1 within picoseconds (Kasha's rule), the fluorophore returns radiatively to a vibrational level of S0 through photon emission at a longer wavelength λem. The resulting bathochromic shift between absorption and emission maxima is known as the Stokes shift. In an excitation scan, the emission monochromator is held at a fixed wavelength while the excitation monochromator sweeps across the absorption region, mirroring the absorption profile. In an emission scan, the excitation monochromator is held fixed while the emission monochromator scans longer wavelengths to resolve the emission spectral envelope. In dilute solutions where optical absorbance A≤0.05, fluorescence intensity varies linearly with concentration according to the Beer-Lambert linearized relation, whereas high concentrations induce non-linear inner-filter attenuation and self-quenching.

  • Linearized Fluorescence Intensity Law:
    Relates measured fluorescence emission intensity to incident excitation intensity, quantum efficiency, molar absorptivity, optical path length, and fluorophore concentration in dilute solutions
    F=2.303·I0·Φf·ϵ·b·c
    Where:
    • F : Measured fluorescence emission intensity (a.u.)
    • I0 : Incident excitation light beam intensity (Wm2)
    • Φf : Fluorescence quantum yield of the fluorophore (dimensionless)
    • ϵ : Molar absorption coefficient of the fluorophore at excitation wavelength (Lmol·cm)
    • b : Optical path length of the sample quartz cuvette (cm)
    • c : Molar concentration of the fluorophore solution (molL)

04 / Procedure

The experiment is conducted by initializing the spectrofluorimeter optics, preparing an optically clear solution of fluorescein, and running both excitation and emission spectral sweeps using right-angle detector geometry to map fluorophore photophysical properties.

  1. Step 1: Power on the spectrofluorimeter and Xenon arc light source, allowing 15 minutes for optical lamp arc stabilization.
  2. Step 2: Prepare a dilute aqueous fluorescein solution in the volumetric flask and inspect the solution for optical clarity.
  3. Step 3: Transfer 3.0 mL of the solution into the 4-sided transparent quartz cuvette using the micropipette, wipe the optical faces clean with lint-free lens paper, and seat the cuvette securely in the thermostatted sample chamber.
  4. Step 4: Close the sample compartment lid tightly to eliminate ambient stray light.
  5. Step 5: Select Excitation Scan mode, fix the emission monochromator at 515 nm, configure the excitation scan range from 300 nm to 600 nm, and execute the automated spectral sweep.
  6. Step 6: Identify the excitation wavelength maximum (490 nm) from the acquired excitation spectrum.
  7. Step 7: Switch to Emission Scan mode, set the excitation monochromator to 425 nm (or 490 nm), set the emission scan range from 435 nm to 700 nm, and execute the emission scan.
  8. Step 8: Identify the fluorescence emission peak (515 nm) and compute the corresponding Stokes shift.

07 / Validation and limits

  • Assumption: The solvent buffer blank exhibits negligible intrinsic fluorescence and Raman scattering across the 300 nm to 700 nm spectral domain.
  • Assumption: The fluorophore solution remains sufficiently dilute (c≤10 μmol/L, absorbance A<0.05) ensuring linear response without primary or secondary inner-filter distortion.
  • Assumption: The quartz cuvette optical walls possess uniform refractive index and superior UV-visible optical transmission exceeding 90%.
  • Assumption: The sample chamber is completely light-tight, preventing ambient stray photons from reaching the photomultiplier tube.
  • Boundary Limit: At fluorophore concentrations exceeding 10 μmol/L, excitation beam attenuation and self-absorption cause severe non-linear flattening of fluorescence peaks.
  • Boundary Limit: Prolonged high-intensity irradiation from the Xenon arc source induces photobleaching of fluorescein molecules over extended scan durations.
  • Boundary Limit: Excessive monochromator slit widths (> 10 nm) degrade spectral resolution and blend adjacent vibronic transition shoulders.
  • Boundary Limit: Temperature variations alter solvent viscosity and collisional non-radiative quenching rates, shifting the apparent quantum yield.
